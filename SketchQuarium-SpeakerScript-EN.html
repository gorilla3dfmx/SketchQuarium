Slide 3 · What is SketchQuarium? (2:00)

The idea is simple, and that's on purpose. You draw an aquarium creature, you place it in the tank, and it comes alive.

Let's walk through the four steps at the top. Step one: you paint a fish, a jellyfish, a plant or a coral in a simple 2D editor, just like in a paint program. Step two: the drawing is turned into a 3D object. Step three: you move the mouse, a preview of your creature follows it through the tank, and a click puts it there. Step four: it lives. Fish wobble their tails, swim in schools and chase food when you feed them. And if you forget, they get hungry.

Why an aquarium and not a shooter or a racing game? Because it contains almost everything a real game needs: a 3D scene, lighting, effects, user input, animation, behavior, and saving and loading. But it stays small enough to explain in one talk.

The three cards at the bottom are the storyline for today. First, ready-made building blocks: water, caustics, physics, bubbles and sound come from Gorilla3D components. Second, the custom content, our own code: drawing, mesh, animation, behavior. And third, persistence: aquariums are saved as XML plus PNG images.

Slide 4 · Architecture in four layers (2:00)

Before we zoom in, the big picture. The application is built in four layers, and you can read them from top to bottom.

At the top, in yellow, the FireMonkey form, Unit1. It's the glue: it holds the 3D scene and the toolbar, and it connects all the parts. Every Delphi developer knows this layer.

Below it, in orange, our application logic: the Aquarium units. Elements and scene, the drawing editor, the placement mode, the template catalog and the editor theme. This is the part I actually wrote.

Then, in teal, the Gorilla3D engine. It provides the viewport, models, materials, render passes, physics, particles, audio and an inventory framework. All of this comes as components you can drop on a form, or as classes you use from code.

And at the bottom, FireMonkey and the RTL: windows, 2D graphics with TBitmap and Skia, timers, the file system and vector math.

Plain words: A 3D engine is the library that turns a description of a scene, meaning objects, lights and a camera, into pictures on screen, many times per second.

The yellow box at the bottom is our roadmap: first we see how much we can click together at design time without code, that's layers three and one. Then we go down into the programmed core, layer two.

Slide 5 · Two worlds: design time & runtime (2:00)

This slide is the guiding principle of the whole talk.

On the left, design time. You drag components from the tool palette, you set properties in the Object Inspector, and you see the 3D scene right inside the IDE. Everything ends up in the .fmx file. This is perfect for the static stage: walls, water, light and effects.

On the right, runtime and code. Everything that is dynamic or depends on data: building a mesh from a drawing, creating and removing objects, behavior like swimming and schooling, and reacting to mouse and timer events. That lives in the .pas units.

The rule of thumb: whatever is fixed at design time, you click. Whatever only appears through user input, or changes every frame, you program.

Plain words: A frame is one single picture. A game draws about 60 of them per second, and that's what makes motion look smooth.

Cue: Point out that this is exactly how Delphi developers already work with VCL and FMX forms. Gorilla3D just extends the idea into 3D.

Slide 6 · Clicking the stage together (3:00)

Now let's build the aquarium itself, without code. Every card on this slide is one component in the form designer. Before we look at them, three words that you will hear all the time in 3D.

Plain words: A mesh is the shape of an object, made of many small triangles, like a wireframe. A material describes the surface: color, texture, shininess, transparency. And a texture is simply an image that is wrapped around the mesh, like a sticker.

The root of everything is the TGorillaViewport. It's the 3D window on the form, and it replaces FireMonkey's own TViewport3D. Every 3D object lives inside it, and it runs the whole rendering pipeline behind the scenes.

Then TGorillaLight. Without light, a 3D scene is black. The light also casts the shadows that give the scene depth.

The glass walls are four flat cubes, grouped together in a TGorillaGroup. The water surface is a TGorillaPlane, a flat mesh. Remember it, it comes back on the next slide.

The floor is a TGorillaTerrain. A terrain is a big mesh whose height comes from a grayscale image, a so-called heightmap: bright pixels become hills, dark pixels become valleys. That gives us the sandy relief of the tank floor.

Around everything sits a TGorillaHemisphere, a half dome with an environment image, so we never look into empty black space.

The rock and the octopus are TGorillaModel components. These are models made in a 3D tool like Blender and loaded from a file. The octopus even comes with its own animation; we just start it.

And finally, the TGorillaAssetsManager. It manages asset packages, which are basically ZIP files containing models, textures and sounds, so the application ships everything in one tidy bundle.

The important point at the bottom: position, size, material and textures of all these objects are set in the Object Inspector. Not a single Create call.

Slide 7 · Effects & passes: water becomes real (4:00)

This is my favorite slide, because here the scene starts to look like real water, and again, without graphics code. To understand it, we need one more idea: the render pass.

Plain words: Normally the engine draws the scene once per frame. A render pass is an extra drawing step. Some passes draw the scene again from a different angle into a hidden image, before the real frame. Others take the finished picture and add an effect on top, after the frame, like an Instagram filter.

Now look at the water. Real water does two things: it mirrors what is above it, and it bends the view of what is below it. Gorilla3D does exactly that with two passes.

The reflection pass draws the scene a second time from a mirrored camera, as if you were looking up from below the water surface. The result is a hidden image of the reflection. The refraction pass draws everything below the surface into another hidden image. That's what you see through the water.

The water material, TGorillaWaterMaterialSource, sits on our water plane and combines both images. It uses a special distortion texture to make them wobble like waves, and a normal map for small surface ripples and sun highlights.

Plain words: A normal map is a texture that fakes tiny bumps on a flat surface by changing how light bounces off it. The geometry stays flat, but it looks detailed.

The underwater pass is one of those after-the-frame effects. It looks at every pixel, checks whether it is below the water line, and adds the blue-green haze and light rays you know from diving.

The caustics are the dancing light patterns on the tank floor. In Gorilla3D they are a material behaviour: a small reusable piece of shader code that you attach to a material, here the floor. One caustics component can light up the floor, the rock and every other material at the same time.

Plain words: A shader is a tiny program that runs on the graphics card for every corner and every pixel of an object. It decides the final color. Gorilla3D writes these for you.

The last three cards are classic game building blocks. The particle emitter produces the rising air bubbles: many tiny sprites, each with its own speed and lifetime. The physics system handles collisions, so objects don't fall through the floor; it can run on the Jolt engine or on the physics engine embedded in Gorilla3D. And the audio manager with a playlist gives us background music and sound effects; here it's FMOD, but OpenAL works too. More on these choices on the next slide.

The key message: all of this, refracted and reflecting water, caustics, bubbles and physics, is composed from components. You connect them by setting properties, like the water material's ReflectionPass property pointing to the reflection pass.

Cue: If you show the IDE, open the water material in the Object Inspector and point at the ReflectionPass and RefractionPass properties.

Slide 8 · Swappable backends (2:00)

Before we leave design time, one more point that's easy to overlook: the engine underneath is exchangeable, in three places. The scene and its components stay exactly the same.

First, graphics. Gorilla3D can render with OpenGL or with Vulkan, and SketchQuarium runs on both. You choose once, in the project's .dpr file: you set GorillaGraphicsBackend from the unit Gorilla.Context.Backend, before Application.Initialize. Without that line you get OpenGL, the platform default. On Windows 64-bit and Linux 64-bit, SketchQuarium switches to Vulkan. Vulkan compiles its shaders at runtime, and that's why shaderc_shared.dll ships next to the EXE. One more call in the .dpr makes laptops with hybrid graphics use the dedicated NVIDIA or AMD chip instead of the integrated one.

Plain words: OpenGL and Vulkan are two different "languages" for talking to the graphics card. OpenGL is older and simpler; Vulkan is newer, closer to the hardware, and usually faster when there are many objects.

Second, physics. The TGorillaPhysicsSystem has a Driver property. JOLTPhysics uses the native Jolt engine, a modern and very fast physics library, through Jolt64.dll. Q3Physics uses the physics engine that is embedded in Gorilla3D, so no extra DLL is needed. The colliders you configured stay the same: it's literally one property in the Object Inspector.

Third, audio. SketchQuarium uses the FMOD audio manager, with a channel group called Effects for the sound effects. Gorilla3D also ships an OpenAL audio manager, and both derive from the same base class, TGorillaAudioManager. Switching means dropping the other component on the form; only the three PlaySound calls in Unit1, which use FMOD's channel groups, would need a small adjustment.

And macOS on Apple Silicon? Gorilla3D supports Metal there in principle. To be honest with you: I haven't tested SketchQuarium on macOS ARM yet, so I can't promise that it runs there today. But nothing in the app is Windows-specific by design.

Cue: For the short version, keep only the graphics paragraph and the macOS note.

Slide 9 · The 2D user interface (1:30)

A game also needs a normal user interface, and here FireMonkey shines. On the left side we have the tool bar, a TRectangle called ToolsArea, with five buttons: draw, feed, inventory, save and load. There's a PopupArea where the drawing editor appears, and an InventoryArea for the template catalog.

The nice part: this 2D interface sits directly on top of the 3D viewport. FireMonkey mixes 2D controls and 3D content in the same form, without any extra work.

The dark "deep sea" look of the editor, with the same colors as these slides, is also set in the designer, in the .fmx. Only a small unit, Aquarium.Theme, adds what you can't click: hover effects and the restyling of a few standard controls. We'll see it in the unit list in a moment.

The note at the bottom repeats our rule: how the buttons look is clicked together. What they do in OnClick is code, and that's where we go next.

Slide 10 · Where code becomes essential (1:30)

So far, everything was static. But the real fun of SketchQuarium is that the content comes from the user, and no form designer can predict that.

Four things need code. First, a drawing becomes a 3D object: the mesh only exists after the user has painted, so it must be built at runtime. Second, we don't know how many fish there will be, so objects are created and freed dynamically. Third, swimming, wobbling, schooling and feeding are calculated frame by frame. And fourth, the state of the aquarium has to be saved and restored.

From here on, we leave the Object Inspector and open the .pas units.

Slide 11 · The units & their responsibilities (2:00)

Here is how the code is organized. Six units, each with one clear job.

Unit1 is the main form. It creates and wires the parts, owns the animation timer and handles button clicks and the feeding mode.

Aquarium.Elements is the heart. TAquariumScene builds the meshes and drives animation and schooling. TSpawnedElement is one living creature in the tank. And TAquariumLibrary manages the templates and saving.

Aquarium.EditorFrame is the drawing editor, built as a FireMonkey frame, with canvas, stencils and color selection. How painting inside the stencils works is the next slide.

Aquarium.Placement is the placement mode. When you move the mouse, it shoots an invisible ray from the camera through the mouse position into the scene and checks what it hits, for example the floor or the rock. That's where the preview appears.

Plain words: A raycast is like a laser pointer: you shoot a straight line into the 3D world and ask, "what do I hit first, and where?" It's how you click on things in 3D.

Aquarium.InventoryUI shows all drawings as tiles; it extends the inventory frame that ships with Gorilla3D.

And Aquarium.Theme gives the editor its dark "deep sea" design. It holds the color constants, which match these slides, adds hover and press effects to the buttons, which are plain TRectangles, and switches toggle states, for example the active eraser or the selected kind. The interesting part is the standard controls: the Windows style draws TEdit, TTrackBar and TListBox from bitmaps that ignore our colors. So in OnPainting, the theme removes those style bitmaps and puts simple shapes in the theme colors in their place, for example a round teal knob on the slider.

The idea at the bottom: what, draw, place, select, style, wire up. One responsibility per unit, so Unit1 stays small.

Slide 12 · Painting inside the shape (3:00)

Now the question I get most often after a demo: how do you actually color inside the fish? In the editor you pick a kind, for example fish, and then one of the shapes in the stencil bar. Then you just paint, and whatever you do, the paint stays inside the outline. Let's see how that works, following the four steps at the top.

Step one: the shapes are ordinary SVG files in the folder assets/shapes, one subfolder per kind: fish, jelly, plant and coral. The editor loads all of them at start-up, and the kind chips filter which ones you see. So a new shape means dropping an SVG file into a folder, no code.

Step two: when you pick a shape, Skia renders it in white onto a 600 by 600 pixel bitmap, exactly the size of the drawing canvas. We only look at the alpha channel of that image and store it as an array of numbers between zero and one, one per pixel. That's the mask: one means inside, zero means outside, and values in between give a soft, smooth edge.

Plain words: Skia is the 2D graphics library behind Chrome and Android, and Delphi ships it. Alpha is the fourth color channel, the transparency: zero is invisible, full is opaque.

Step three: from the mask we compute the outline you see on the canvas. Every inside pixel that has an outside neighbor becomes part of a three-pixel line. It's drawn in a separate TImage on top of the canvas, so it guides you, but it is never saved into your drawing.

Step four is the actual trick. You paint normally into the bitmap, with any brush. Right after each stroke segment, ApplyStencilClip walks over the small rectangle around that segment and compares every pixel with the mask. If the pixel is more opaque than the mask allows, its alpha is capped to the mask value. Outside the shape that value is zero, so the paint disappears. At the soft edge it becomes partly transparent. We only ever lower alpha, never add, so painting over the edge twice doesn't make it darker. That's the code on the right.

On the left, the four brushes. The round brush is simply FireMonkey's Canvas.DrawLine with round caps. The soft brushes compute a smooth falloff per pixel. To avoid color building up where a stroke crosses itself, they keep a snapshot of the image from the start of the stroke, remember the highest coverage per pixel, and always blend from that snapshot. The elongated brush stamps rotated ellipses at a fixed 45 degree angle, like a calligraphy pen. The eraser sets pixels back to transparent. And Freehand means no stencil at all: draw any shape you like.

The result is a 600 by 600 bitmap with a transparent background. And that transparency is exactly what becomes the silhouette of the creature in 3D, as we'll see in two slides.

Cue: If you can, demo this live: choose the batfish shape, scribble wildly across the outline with the soft brush, and show that nothing leaks out.

Slide 13 · From stroke to mesh (3:00)

Now the most interesting technical step: how does a flat drawing become a 3D object?

Follow the pipeline at the top. The editor gives us the TBitmap with its alpha channel, so the background is transparent. The function BuildModelDef turns it into a TModelDef. LoadNewModelFromDef creates a real TGorillaModel from that. And finally it becomes a TSpawnedElement in the scene, which can live and swim.

The key concept here is the model definition, TModelDef. Think of it as a blueprint, or a recipe: it describes the geometry, the material and the animation, but it's not yet visible. Gorilla3D then builds a real model from this blueprint. The same idea is used when Gorilla3D loads a model file: the file is first read into a definition, and then the model is built from it.

The code shows the geometry part. We build a flat square, a quad, and divide it into a 24 by 24 grid. For every grid point we set two things. A position in space, from minus one half to plus one half. And a texture coordinate, also called UV, from zero to one.

Plain words: UV coordinates tell the graphics card which point of the image belongs to which point of the mesh. U is left to right, V is top to bottom. Here it's a one-to-one mapping, so the drawing is stretched exactly over the square.

Why a grid and not just two triangles? Because a flat card can't bend. With 25 times 25 points we can later move each point individually, and that's how the fish wobbles its tail. Keep that in mind for slides 15 and 16.

Slide 14 · The sprite material (2:30)

We have a shape. Now it needs a surface, and our goal is: the drawing should look like a sticker floating in the water.

That means four things, listed on the left. Unlit, so the painted colors stay exactly as drawn. Double-sided, because a fish turns around, and we want to see it from the back too. Alpha cutout: transparent parts of the drawing are cut away, so you see the fish shape and not a square. And the texture comes from the drawn bitmap at runtime.

Plain words: A sprite is a flat image that is placed in a 3D world. Old 2D games were made entirely of sprites.

The code on the right does exactly that on the material definition: UseLighting false, DoubleSided true, UseAlphaCutoff true with a threshold of 0.1. Then we import the bitmap as a texture asset and attach it as the diffuse, meaning the base color, channel.

And now a real story from development, the "black fish" in the yellow box. Everything looked fine, until a fish turned around, and it was suddenly black. The reason: Gorilla3D enables lighting internally when the model is built. The back side faced away from the light, so it got dark. The solution: after building the model, we walk through its materials and switch lighting off again. Lesson learned: sometimes an engine is smarter than you want it to be.

Slide 15 · Animation on two levels (2:00)

A fish needs two kinds of movement, and the trick is to keep them separate.

On the left, the own animation, the wobbling. This is baked vertex animation.

Plain words: A vertex is one corner point of the mesh, one of our grid points. Vertex animation moves these points over time. Baked means we calculate all the steps once in advance and store them, so at runtime the engine only plays them back, like a flip book.

For a fish we compute a travelling wave along the body, stronger at the tail fin. There are two loops: "self", the calm swimming, and "fast", for hunting food. The engine plays them back, very cheaply. How exactly that wave is computed is the next slide.

On the right, movement through space, the swimming. That's not part of the mesh at all. Our code moves the whole model every frame based on a velocity, and turns it so it looks where it's going. This is the foundation for schooling and chasing food.

Because the two are separate, we can change direction and speed freely, and the tail keeps wobbling naturally.

One practical note in the box: the loop is part of the definition itself, the interpolator is marked as looping. So at runtime there's nothing to set up; switching between the calm and the fast loop is a single call, PlayAnimation with the name of the loop.

Slide 16 · The fish wobble, computed (3:00)

Let's open the left box of the previous slide: how exactly is the wobble computed? It's one function, DeformedVertex, and a small baking loop.

The function gets one grid point, u and v, each between minus a half and plus a half, and an angle theta that runs once around the circle during one loop. It returns where that point should be at that moment.

For a fish, first the tail weight: w equals one half minus u. Our fish faces right, so the tail fin is at u equals minus a half, where w is one, and the head is at plus a half, where w is zero. The head stays calm, the tail moves.

Then the travelling wave: the sine of six u minus speed times theta. Because u is inside the sine, neighboring points are slightly out of step, so the wave runs along the body, like a real fish. The amplitude is multiplied by w to the power of tailPow: the higher that exponent, the more the motion concentrates at the fin.

The wave moves the point mainly along Z, that's the fin beat. Because the fish is a flat card facing the camera, Z points mostly toward you or away from you. So we add a small part in Y, in the picture plane, so you can see the swimming from the front as well.

One detail matters a lot: speed must be a whole number. Theta runs from zero to two pi. If speed is an integer, the wave at the end of the loop is exactly the wave at the start, and the loop has no visible jump.

Plain words: A seamless loop is an animation whose last frame leads perfectly into its first, so it can repeat forever without a hiccup.

Jellyfish, plants and corals use the same function with different formulas: the jellyfish bell pulses and its hem trails behind; plants and corals sway with their root fixed, corals stiffer than plants.

Now the baking, on the right. AddWobble samples theta at 25 evenly spaced points. For each sample it calls DeformedVertex for all 625 grid points and stores the complete vertex array as one keyframe in a TMeshInterpolatorDef, with linear blending in between. That happens once per drawing: every fish made from the same template shares it, and at runtime the engine only blends between two stored keyframes.

The table shows the two fish loops. "self" is calm: 1.6 seconds, one wave per loop. "fast" is the hunting mode: 0.85 seconds, two waves per loop, 40 percent more amplitude, and more emphasis on the tail. Its ramp of 0.6 lets the beat swell towards the end of the loop and fall back to normal exactly at the loop point, so it still loops seamlessly.

Switching is a single property: setting FastSwim to true calls AnimationManager.PlayAnimation with "fast". The scene does that automatically when a fish chases food, and switches back when it has eaten.

Cue: For the short version, skip the parameter table and the ramp.

Slide 17 · Life in the scene: schools & food (2:30)

Now the fish can move. But how do they decide where to go? This is where it gets lively.

In SteerFish we use a classic model called boids, invented in 1986 by Craig Reynolds to simulate flocks of birds. The surprising thing is that you don't program the swarm. You give each fish three simple rules, and the swarm emerges by itself.

Rule one, separation: keep your distance. Every fish is pushed away from all other fish, and the closer they are, the stronger the push. Rule two, alignment: swim in the same direction as your neighbors, but only neighbors of the same species. Rule three, cohesion: move toward the center of your group, again only your own species. So red fish school with red fish, and blue fish with blue fish, but nobody bumps into anybody.

Each rule produces a small steering force, they are added up, and that changes the fish's velocity. That's the whole magic.

On top of that, feeding. Click on the feed button, then into the tank, and DropFood drops a sinking pellet. Only hungry fish go for it: AssignFood gives each of them one pellet, they switch to their fast animation and eat it. Why only hungry fish? That's the next slide.

Jellyfish are simpler: they gently wander in 3D and bob up and down. And everybody bounces off the tank walls, so nobody escapes.

Slide 18 · Hunger, health & death (2:00)

Feeding alone is a nice effect. It becomes a game with one question: what happens if you don't feed them?

Every fish now has health, from 0 to 100. After a meal it stays full for 15 seconds. Then it gets hungry and loses half a point per second, so from full to dead takes a bit more than three minutes. Every pellet gives back 30 points.

The four boxes at the top are the stages. A sated fish ignores the food. Below 40 points it is hungry and slowly pulses in a pale green. Below 15 it is critical and blinks fast and dark: that's your last warning. At zero it dies. It turns grey, stops wobbling, flips belly-up and drifts to the surface. Twelve seconds later it's gone.

Who gets the food when several fish want it? AssignFood gives each pellet to exactly one fish, and each fish gets at most one pellet. The score is the distance minus a bonus for every missing health point. So a fish that is almost dead beats a well-fed one that is closer, and ten fish no longer fight over one pellet.

Health is saved with each fish, like position and size. A hungry aquarium stays hungry after loading.

Now the yellow box. There is no health bar in the UI: the fish itself is the display. We tint its material, and because every model has its own material source, only this one fish changes color.

Plain words: Tinting means multiplying the colors of the texture by a color. White keeps the drawing as it is, green makes it look sick.

Cue: For the Delphi experts, the pitfall in the box: with lighting off, the final color is texture times the sum of ambient, diffuse and specular. Lowering only diffuse barely shows, so the code moves the whole sum into diffuse and blends that toward the target color.

Cue: For a live demo, raise FISH_HUNGER_RATE so a fish starves within a minute.

Slide 19 · Inventory & persistence (2:00)

An aquarium you can't save is no fun. For this, I didn't write my own file format; I reused the Gorilla3D inventory system.

The inventory is actually made for games: weapons, keys, potions. It follows a template and instance pattern. A template is the blueprint of an item, and a collectable is one concrete item a player owns. That fits our aquarium perfectly: the drawing is the template, and each placed fish is a collectable. Templates are grouped by species.

The drawing's texture is attached to the template as a bitmap and also stored as a PNG file. TAquariumLibrary is just a thin facade on top of the inventory.

Saving, on the right: first, each fish's position, size and health is written into the collectable's parameters. Then SaveToFile writes everything into one XML file, plus the PNG images.

Loading goes the other way: clear the scene, load the XML, and RestoreFromInventory rebuilds every model from its template, with exactly the same mesh and material code we saw before.

The lesson here: before you write your own solution, look at what the framework already offers. Often it's meant for something else, but fits better than expected.

Slide 20 · The animation loop (1:30)

The last building block is the heartbeat of every game: the loop.

A simple FireMonkey TTimer fires roughly every 16 milliseconds, that's about 60 times per second. On each tick, Scene.Animate calculates the behavior: the boid rules, movement, food and hunger. That's the only active line in the timer event.

And who draws the picture? The viewport does that on its own. In the Object Inspector, the Gorilla viewport has UseFixedFrameRate set to true and FixedFrameRate set to 60. The engine then renders the scene, plays the wobble animations, draws the water and runs all render passes at a steady 60 frames per second, by itself.

You only need to call Viewport.Invalidate after Animate if fixed frame rate rendering is deactivated. That's the commented-out line in the code, with exactly that note.

One important detail, mentioned at the bottom. Animate gets the real elapsed time from a clock, and calculates the time since the last frame, called delta time.

Plain words: Delta time, or dt, is the time that passed since the last frame. If you move a fish by "speed times dt" instead of a fixed step per frame, it swims at the same speed on a fast gaming PC and on a slow laptop.

Without delta time, the fish would swim twice as fast on a computer that calculates twice as many frames. With it, the behavior is the same everywhere.

Slide 21 · Lessons & best practices (2:00)

Let me sum up what I learned while building this, as six practical tips.

One: design time first. Compose as much as you can from components, and write code only for the dynamic parts. That saved me an enormous amount of work.

Two: understand who owns what. By default a model owns its model definition and frees it when it's destroyed. In SketchQuarium all fish of one drawing share one cached definition, so we set IsDefOwner to false, and the scene frees the definitions itself, once no fish uses them any more. If the model kept ownership, the first fish you remove would free the definition for all the others, and you'd get an access violation. Classic Delphi ownership, just in 3D.

Three: the lighting trap, our black fish. If you want the sprite look, switch lighting off after the model is built, not before.

Four: seamless loops. Every time term in a baked animation must be a whole multiple of the loop angle. Then the last keyframe equals the first, and the loop never jumps.

Five: separate responsibilities. Drawing, placement, scene, catalog and theme each in their own unit. That's not 3D-specific, it's simply good architecture, and it matters even more in a game, because games grow fast.

Six: one timer, one delta time. Base all movement on time, not on frames.

Slide 22 · Summary & questions (1:00)

To wrap up, six takeaways.

Delphi plus Gorilla3D combines visual composition with full control in code. The static stage, meaning water, caustics, physics, bubbles and sound, is created almost entirely at design time. The creative core, from drawing to mesh to animation to schooling to saving, is cleanly encapsulated code. The model definition approach is the key to turning user content into real 3D objects at runtime. Two separate animation levels plus a time-based loop make the world feel alive. And underneath, the backends are swappable: OpenGL or Vulkan, Jolt or Q3, FMOD or OpenAL.

If you take one thing home: 3D development with Delphi doesn't start with shaders and math. It starts with components, just like any other Delphi app. And you add code exactly where your idea needs it.

Prepared answers for likely questions

Do I need to know shaders or math to start? No. Materials, render passes and behaviours ship as components. You only touch shader code if you want your own effects.

How do I color inside the fish – and can I add my own shapes? Pick a shape in the stencil bar and paint; every stroke is clipped to the shape's mask (slide 12). Your own shapes are plain SVG files: drop one into assets/shapes/fish, jelly, plant or coral, and it appears after the next start.

Which platforms are supported? Gorilla3D's platform guides cover Windows, Linux, macOS and Android. SketchQuarium is developed and tested on Windows 64-bit, and set up to use Vulkan on Linux 64-bit as well.

Does it run on a Mac with Apple Silicon and Metal? Gorilla3D supports Metal in principle. I haven't tested SketchQuarium on macOS ARM yet, so I can't confirm it today, but nothing in the app is Windows-specific by design.

OpenGL or Vulkan? Both. You choose the graphics backend in the project file with the unit Gorilla.Context.Backend, before Application.Initialize; without it you get OpenGL. SketchQuarium uses Vulkan on Windows 64-bit and Linux 64-bit, needs shaderc_shared.dll for that, and forces the dedicated GPU on laptops with hybrid graphics.

Do I need a DLL for physics? Not necessarily. The Jolt driver uses Jolt64.dll; the embedded Q3 engine needs none. You switch with the Driver property of TGorillaPhysicsSystem, and the colliders stay the same.

Can I use OpenAL instead of FMOD? Yes. Use TGorillaOpenALManager instead of TGorillaFMODAudioManager; both derive from TGorillaAudioManager. In SketchQuarium only the three PlaySound calls that use FMOD channel groups would need adjusting.

How many fish can it handle? The wobble animation is baked once per drawing and played back by the engine, so it's cheap. The boid rules compare every fish with every other fish, so the cost grows with the square of the fish count. For a hobby aquarium that's fine; for thousands of fish you would use spatial partitioning.

Can I load models from Blender? Yes. Gorilla3D imports common formats such as glTF and FBX, plus its own G3D format, and the AssetsManager bundles them into packages.
